How Faal writes Persian in Roman letters

The Roman line under each couplet in Faal is a pronunciation guide for readers of the Persianate tradition in Pakistan and India, not a scholarly transliteration. It records how such a reader says a line of Hafez, so that someone who cannot read the Persian script can say the couplet aloud. The Persian text is always on the same screen.

The reader

We write for a reader in Pakistan or India with an interest in Persian literary culture, who reads Urdu or Hindi comfortably and Persian script slowly or not at all. Such a reader already knows dil, gul, sāqī, jām and hijr, says vafā rather than wafā, and reads شعر as sher.

Where we agree with the academic standard (IJMES)

  • Long vowels carry macrons: ā, ī, ū.
  • Short vowels are i and u, not the modern Iranian e and o: dil, gul, lutf, not del, gol, loft.
  • The contracted conjunction is wa-.
  • Classical vocabulary and forms are kept, and one system is used throughout.

Where we depart from it, and why

The vowels ē and ō. We write dōsh, nēst, gētī, khurshēd, where IJMES writes dūsh, nīst, gītī, khurshīd. Classical Persian distinguished these "majhūl" vowels from ī and ū. Modern Iranian Persian lost the distinction; the Persianate traditions of India, Pakistan and Afghanistan kept it. Writing ē and ō is closer both to Hafez's own pronunciation and to how our readers recite him.

و as v. We write vafā, muravvat, vaqt. Some scholarly works covering the whole Persianate world use w; for readers in Pakistan and India, و is not lip-rounded in these words, and v is the closer guide. Exceptions: khw- stays (khwāb, khwēsh), Arabic phrases keep w, and a final و is a vowel (khusrau, rehrau).

The ezafe as -e. We write lutf-e azal, shab-e qadr, and after a vowel -ye: parda-ye ghaib. Urdu says -e; -i would be read wrongly.

Colouring before h. We write shehr, gireh, sohbat, rehmat, zehmat, where IJMES writes shahr, girih, ṣuḥbat, raḥmat, zaḥmat. This is how an Urdu speaker says a vowel before a closing h. Colouring changes the vowel but never adds one, so شهد is shehd, and the line still matches the Persian syllable for syllable. It applies word by word, not as a blanket rule: صحبت is sohbat but صحرا stays sahrā, and سحر is sahar for dawn and sehr for magic. Names keep their familiar forms (ahriman, Khusrau, Qārūn).

Arabic consonants are not marked. We write hadīs, ta'sīr, sabr, zulmat rather than ḥadīṯ, taʾthīr, ṣabr, ẓulmat. Urdu does not distinguish these sounds in speech, and dots would ask the reader to make a distinction they do not make. The information is not lost: the Persian script is always alongside.

Prefixes as they are said: ba- rather than bi-, mē- rather than mī-.

Three scripts, three systems

Each couplet appears in Persian, in Roman letters, in Urdu and in Devanagari. These are not four copies of one spelling. The Roman shows pronunciation; Devanagari follows ordinary Hindi spelling, which shows the colouring in मेहर and सोहबत but not in शहर or रहम. Both are correct as they stand.

Urdu Roman Devanagari
شہر shehr शहर
زحمت zehmat ज़हमत
گرہ gireh गिरह
مہر mehr मेहर
صحبت sohbat सोहबत

The text

The Persian follows the Qazvini–Ghani edition, as carried by the Ganjoor open library. Where a vowel mark in the source and the reading disagree, we check the edition rather than adjust the Roman quietly.

A scholarly transliteration may follow one day, as a separate line for readers who want it, never replacing this one.
